Chapter 0

Pronunciation & Jyutping Fundamentals

Master the mechanical foundations of spoken Cantonese, including the six core tones, vowel length distinctions, silent stop consonants, and essential conversational exclamations.

Welcome to spoken Cantonese! Unlike Mandarin, which has four tones and is heavily standardized, Colloquial Cantonese has six distinct tones and preserves ancient phonetic endings (consonant stops) that give the language its unique rhythmic, staccato flavor.

In this foundational chapter, we will demystify the sound system using LSHK Jyutping, the standard romanization system for Cantonese.

1. The Six Tones

Cantonese is a pitch-sensitive tonal language. The exact same syllable, pronounced with a different pitch or contour, will have a completely different meaning.

There are six standard tones, which can be categorized by their pitch heights (High, Mid, Low) and their movements (Flat/Level vs. Rising vs. Falling):

  • Tone 1 (High Flat / 55): High level pitch. Sounds like saying "Aha!" in realization.
    • Example: 詩si1
      poem / poetry
  • Tone 2 (High Rising / 35): Starts mid, rises high. Sounds like asking a question: "What?"
    • Example: 史si2
      history
  • Tone 3 (Mid Flat / 33): Neutral, mid level. Sounds like stating a matter-of-fact: "Okay."
    • Example: 試si3
      to try / test
  • Tone 4 (Low Falling / 21): Low, dropping pitch. Sounds like grunting in disappointment: "Oh..."
    • Example: 時si4
      time / hour
  • Tone 5 (Low Rising / 23): Starts low, rises slightly. Sounds like a hesitant question: "Really?"
    • Example: 市si5
      market / city
  • Tone 6 (Low Flat / 22): Deep, flat level pitch. Sounds like a low, assertive monotone: "Yep."
    • Example: 事si6
      matter / business / affair

The "si" Tone Series

To practice hearing the six tones sequentially, native speakers often recite the classic "si" sequence. Practice repeating these six words in order:

詩si1
poem 史si2
history 試si3
to try 時si4
time 市si5
market 事si6
matter
Poem, history, to try, time, market, matter

2. Vowel Length Distinction: aa vs a

In Cantonese, the length of a vowel is semantically significant. This is especially true for the letter A:

  • aa is a long vowel (pronounced open and long, like the "ah" in "father").
  • a is a short vowel (pronounced clipped and short, like the "u" in "cup").

If you pronounce a long vowel short, or vice versa, you will say a completely different word:

Long Vowel aa

  • 街gaai1
    street    : Pronounced with a long, open "ah" sound (rhymes with English "guy").
  • 沙saa1
    sand    : Pronounced with a long, drawn-out "sah" sound.

Short Vowel a

  • 雞gai1
    chicken    : Pronounced with a short, clipped "uh-ee" sound (close to English "gay").
  • 新san1
    new    : Pronounced with a short, abrupt "suhn" sound.

Observe the vowel length contrast in this simple phrase:

街gaai1
street 雞gai1
chicken
Street chicken

3. Silent Stop Consonant Endings (-p, -t, -k)

In English, when a word ends in p, t, or k (e.g., "cup", "cat", "back"), speakers naturally release a small puff of air at the end of the consonant.

In Cantonese, these final consonants are silent stops. You must form the shape of the consonant with your mouth, but hold the air and do not release it:

  • -p Ending: Close your lips fully to block the air, but do not make a "puh" sound.
    • Example: 十sap6
      ten       (Pronounce "suh..." and seal your lips shut immediately).
  • -t Ending: Place your tongue behind your teeth to block the air, but do not make a "tuh" sound.
    • Example: 日jat6
      day / sun       in 今日gam1jat6
      today      .
  • -k Ending: Block the air at the back of your throat, but do not make a "kuh" sound.
    • Example: 食sik6
      to eat      .

Practice pronouncing these unreleased stops naturally:

我ngo5
I 想soeng2
want to 食sik6
to eat 十sap6
ten 隻zek3
classifier for animals 雞gai1
chicken。
I want to eat ten chickens.

4. Spoken Exclamations: 喂wai2
hey and 吓haa2
what

Cantonese conversations are incredibly dynamic and expressive, filled with emotional particles and exclamations that immediately convey the speaker's tone. Two of the most high-frequency exclamations are:

  1. 喂wai2
    hey    : Used to grab someone's attention, answer the phone ("hello?"), or express mild, friendly annoyance.
  2. 吓haa2
    what    : Used to express surprise, confusion, disbelief, or to ask someone to repeat themselves.

Observe how these are naturally integrated in a conversational exchange:

B:

not 食sik6
to eat 啦laa1
particle，我ngo5
I 唔m4
not 食sik6
to eat 事si6
matter 嘅ge3
particle。
What? No, I don't eat it.

5. Exercises

Test your understanding of the tones, vowel lengths, and stop endings introduced in this chapter:

Practice Exercise

Which tone number represents the Low Falling pitch contour (e.g. 時si4
time)?
A) Tone 1
B) Tone 3
C) Tone 4
D) Tone 6

Reveal Answer & Explanation
Answer: C

Tone 4 is the Low Falling tone (pitch contour 21), which sounds like a disappointed grunt "oh...".
