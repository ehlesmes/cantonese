Chapter 17

Modals of Ability & Potential Suffixes

Master the modal verbs sik, ho2ji5, and wui5 for expressing capability and permission, and learn to use potential suffixes dak and m4dou2.

In this chapter, we will learn how to express different types of capability and permission using Cantonese modal verbs, and how to use potential suffixes directly with verbs to indicate physical feasibility.

Let's dive in!

1. The Three Faces of "Can": 識sik1
to know / speak (a language) / have a skill, 可以ho2ji5
can / may / possible, and 會wui5
will / would / can / be able to

In English, we use the word "can" to cover many meanings. In Cantonese, you must choose the correct modal verb based on the type of capability.

1. Learned Skills and Abilities: 識sik1
to know / speak (a language) / have a skill

Use 識sik1
to know / speak (a language) / have a skill when "can" means you know how to do something because you learned it. This is used for languages, sports, driving, cooking, and academic subjects.

  • 我ngo5
    I / me     識sik1
    to know / speak (a language) / have a skill     講gong2
    to speak / say / talk     廣東話gwong2dung1waa2
    Cantonese language     — I can (know how to) speak Cantonese.
  • 我ngo5
    I / me     識sik1
    to know / speak (a language) / have a skill     講gong2
    to speak / say / talk     英文jing1man2
    English language     — I can (know how to) speak English.
  • 我ngo5
    I / me     識sik1
    to know / speak (a language) / have a skill     揸車zaa1ce1
    to drive a car / drive     — I can (know how to) drive a car.

To negate this, place 唔m4
not / not / no (negative prefix) before 識sik1
to know / speak (a language) / have a skill:

我ngo5
I / me 唔m4
not / not / no (negative prefix) 識sik1
to know / speak (a language) / have a skill 寫se2
to write 英文jing1man2
English language 呀aa3
sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly。
I do not know how to write English.

2. Situational Permission and Possibility: 可以ho2ji5
can / may / possible

Use 可以ho2ji5
can / may / possible when "can" means you are permitted to do something or it is situationally possible (e.g. schedules align).

  • 你nei5
    you     可以ho2ji5
    can / may / possible     喺hai2
    at / in / on (locative preposition)     呢度ni1dou6
    here     飲jam2
    to drink     咖啡gaa3fe1
    coffee (loanword)     — You can (are allowed to) drink coffee here.
  • 我ngo5
    I / me     今日gam1jat6
    today     可以ho2ji5
    can / may / possible     去heoi3
    to go     旅行leoi5hang4
    to travel     — I can (am able to situationally) go travel today.

To negate permission or situational ability, place 唔m4
not / not / no (negative prefix) before 可以ho2ji5
can / may / possible:

呢度ni1dou6
here 唔m4
not / not / no (negative prefix) 可以ho2ji5
can / may / possible 揸車zaa1ce1
to drive a car / drive 囉lo1
sentence-final particle showing obviousness, resignation, or 'of course'！
You cannot drive a car here, obviously!

3. Future Likelihood and Tendencies: 會wui5
will / would / can / be able to

Use 會wui5
will / would / can / be able to to express future actions ("will") or an inherent likelihood/tendency that something will happen.

  • 我ngo5
    I / me     聽日ting1jat6
    tomorrow     會wui5
    will / would / can / be able to     嚟lei4
    to come     — I will come tomorrow.
我ngo5
I / me 唔m4
not / not / no (negative prefix) 會wui5
will / would / can / be able to 去heoi3
to go 日本jat6bun2
Japan 啦laa1
sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action。
I will not go to Japan.

2. Physical Feasibility and Obstacles: Potential Suffixes 得dak1
particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree, 唔得m4dak1
cannot / not allowed / physically bad to do, and 唔到m4dou2
cannot / unable to manage to / fail to

In daily spoken Cantonese, instead of using helper words like 可以ho2ji5
can / may / possible, native speakers prefer to attach suffixes directly to the end of verbs to show whether an action is physically possible.

1. Capability & Physical Feasibility: 得dak1
particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree

When you attach 得dak1
particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree directly to the end of a verb, it indicates that the action is physically ddoable, allowed, or ready:

Formula: [Verb] + 得dak1
particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree

  • 食sik6
    to eat     得dak1
    particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree     — edible / ready to eat. (e.g. 呢個ni1go3
    this one     食sik6
    to eat     得dak1
    particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree     — This is ready to eat!)
  • 去heoi3
    to go     得dak1
    particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree     — ready to go / able to go.
  • 行hang4
    to walk / go / run (a machine)     得dak1
    particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree     — able to walk / ready to walk.
我ngo5
I / me 依家ji1gaa1
now / currently 去heoi3
to go 得dak1
particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree 啦laa1
sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action！
I can go now! / I am ready to go now!

2. Lack of Permission or Suitability: 唔m4
not...得dak1
particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree

To express that an action is not allowed, forbidden, or physically bad/unsuitable to do, place the negation particle 唔m4
not before the verb, followed by 得dak1
particle expressing permission/possibility:

Formula: 唔m4
not / no (negative prefix) + [Verb] + 得dak1
particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree

  • 唔m4
    not     食sik6
    to eat     得dak1
    particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree     — not allowed to eat / shouldn't be eaten.
  • 唔m4
    not     去heoi3
    to go     得dak1
    particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree     — not allowed to go.

3. Ability and Inability to Succeed: 到dou2
can manage to / successfully do and 唔到m4dou2
cannot / unable to manage to / fail to

When you want to describe whether you successfully manage to do something (overcoming physical obstacles or reaching a result), you attach the potential suffix 到dou2
resultative complement indicating success / can manage to directly to the verb. Conversely, if you fail to manage or cannot complete the action, you attach 唔到m4dou2
cannot / unable to manage to / fail to:

  • 睇tai2
    to look / see / watch / read     到dou2
    resultative complement indicating success / can manage to     — able to see / successfully saw.
  • 睇tai2
    to look / see / watch / read     唔到m4dou2
    cannot / unable to manage to / fail to     — unable to see (e.g., someone is blocking you).
  • 聽teng1
    to listen / hear / obey     到dou2
    resultative complement indicating success / can manage to     — able to hear / successfully heard.
  • 聽teng1
    to listen / hear / obey     唔到m4dou2
    cannot / unable to manage to / fail to     — unable to hear.
  • 食sik6
    to eat     唔到m4dou2
    cannot / unable to manage to / fail to     — unable to eat (e.g., because you are too full or the restaurant is closed).
  • 去heoi3
    to go     唔到m4dou2
    cannot / unable to manage to / fail to     — unable to go.
TIP

唔得m4dak1
cannot vs 唔到m4dou2
cannot

  • Use the structure 唔m4
    not     + [Verb] + 得dak1
    particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree     when an action is not allowed or bad for you (e.g., 唔m4
    not     食sik6
    to eat     得dak1
    particle expressing permission, possibility, capability, or adverbial degree     means it's toxic, expired, or forbidden to eat).
  • Use 唔到m4dou2
    cannot / unable to manage to / fail to     when you fail to manage or physically cannot complete the action (e.g., 食sik6
    to eat     唔到m4dou2
    cannot / unable to manage to / fail to     means you want to eat it, but you are too full, have no teeth, or the kitchen is closed).
