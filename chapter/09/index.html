Chapter 9

Split-Verbs & Native Idioms

Master the structural flexibility of Cantonese split-verbs like jau4seoi2 and coeng3go1. Learn how to parse and separate them with aspect markers, and discover colorful native idioms and expressions like ceoi1seoi2 and caau2jau4jyu2.

Welcome to Chapter 9! In this chapter, we will explore one of the most structurally unique and culturally vibrant areas of Colloquial Cantonese: Split-Verbs and Native Idioms.

First, we will study Split-Verbs (linguistically known as separable verbs or lei-hap-ci in Cantonese). These words translate as simple single verbs in English (e.g. "to swim" or "to sing"), but structurally they are compound verb-object pairs. To express ongoing actions, past completions, or duration, Cantonese speakers literally split these compounds in half and insert grammar markers directly between them.

Second, we will learn some of the most widely used and colorful Cantonese Native Idioms (俗語zuk1jyu5
colloquial expression / slang / idiom). Many of these idioms are structurally split-verbs, which means they follow the exact same grammatical rules of separation!

Let's dive in!

1. Structuring Split-Verbs: Insert in the Middle

In Cantonese, split-verbs are composed of two parts:

  1. A Verb Part (the action)
  2. An Object Part (the target of the action)

For example, 游水jau4seoi2
to swim is made of 游jau4
to swim and 水seoi2
water. Similarly, 唱歌coeng3go1
to sing is made of 唱coeng3
to sing and 歌go1
song.

When you want to attach aspect markers—such as completed 咗zo2
verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect), continuous 緊gan2
verbal aspect suffix indicating an action in progress (continuous aspect), or experiential 過gwo3
verbal aspect suffix indicating past experience / 'have done before'—you must split the compound and insert the marker between the two parts:

4. Asking A-not-A Questions with Split-Verbs

When forming A-not-A questions (Verb-not-Verb) with split-verbs, you must only duplicate the verb part, not the entire compound. It is a very common learner mistake to say 游水唔游水jau4seoi2 m4 jau4seoi2
swim or not swim (swim-water-not-swim-water)—this is incorrect!

The correct structure is to repeat the verb part, and leave the object part at the end:

However, if you are asking about past completion or past experience involving an aspect marker (like 咗zo2
completed or 過gwo3
past experience), you generally cannot use the A-not-A verb duplication. Instead, use 有冇jau5 mou5
have or have not. For example, 游過水jau4gwo3seoi2
swum becomes 有冇游過水jau5mou5jau4gwo3seoi2
have you swum, not 游唔游過水jau4m4jau4gwo3seoi2
have you swum.

you 今晚gam1maan5
tonight 唱唔唱coeng3 m4 coeng3

2. Modifying Split-Verbs: Time, Duration & Frequency

When you want to describe how long (duration) or how many times (frequency) you performed a split-verb action, the description of time or frequency must also split the verb and be placed between the two halves:

1. Specifying Duration

To say you performed an action for "one hour" (一jat1
one 個go3
general measure word / individual / unit 鐘頭zung1tau4
hour), place the duration immediately after the verb part:

佢keoi5
he / she / it 游jau4
to swim 咗zo2
verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect) 兩loeng5
two / pair (used with classifiers) 個go3
general measure word / individual / unit 鐘頭zung1tau4
hour 水seoi2
water 呀aa3
sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly。
He swam for two hours.

2. Specifying Frequency

To say you performed an action "three times" (三saam1
three 次ci3

to sing 咗zo2
verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect) 三saam1

time / occurrence (classifier) 歌go1

sentence-final particle indicating a change of state or already！
I have sung three times already!

3. Spoken Cantonese Native Idioms

Many of the most popular colloquial expressions (俗語zuk1jyu5
colloquial expression / slang / idiom) used by Hong Kongers in daily life are structurally split-verbs. Let's study five of the most expressive idioms:

1. Chatting & Gossiping: 吹水ceoi1seoi2
to chat / shoot the breeze / gossip

  • Literal meaning: "Blowing water"
  • Colloquial usage: Chattering, hanging out, or gossiping casually.
  • Splitting example:
我哋ngo5dei6
we / us 喺hai2
at / in / on (locative preposition) 茶餐廳caa4caan1teng1
Hong Kong style café / diner 吹ceoi1
to blow / boast 緊gan2
verbal aspect suffix indicating an action in progress (continuous aspect) 水seoi2
water 呀aa3
sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly。
We are shooting the breeze at the Hong Kong style café.

2. Pretending or Showing Off: 扮嘢baan6je5
to pretend / show off / act

  • Literal meaning: "Dressing up things"
  • Colloquial usage: Pretending, showing off, playing dumb, or acting like someone you're not.
  • Splitting example:
佢keoi5
he / she / it 真係zan1hai6
really / truly 扮baan6
to dress up / act / pretend 緊gan2
verbal aspect suffix indicating an action in progress (continuous aspect) 嘢je5
thing / stuff / matter 囉lo1
sentence-final particle showing obviousness, resignation, or 'of course'！
He is really just pretending / showing off, obviously!

3. Standing Someone Up: 放飛機fong3fei1gei1
to stand someone up / break a promise

  • Literal meaning: "Releasing an airplane"
  • Colloquial usage: Standing someone up, failing to show up to an agreed meeting or appointment.
  • Splitting example:
你nei5
you 今日gam1jat6
today 又jau6
again 放fong3
to release / let go / put 我ngo5
I / me 飛機fei1gei1
airplane 喇喎laa3wo3
sentence-final particle combination indicating warning, sudden realization, or change of state！
Hey, you stood me up again today! (Friendly or direct warning!)

4. Sitting Back and Watching Drama: 食花生sik6faa1sang1
to sit back and watch drama / spectate

  • Literal meaning: "Eating peanuts"
  • Colloquial usage: Staying out of a conflict or dramatic situation, just sitting back to enjoy the drama unfold (like eating popcorn at a movie).
  • Splitting example:
我ngo5
I / me 喺hai2
at / in / on (locative preposition) 隔離gaak3lei4
next to / beside / neighbor 食sik6
to eat 緊gan2
verbal aspect suffix indicating an action in progress (continuous aspect) 花生faa1sang1
peanut 啫ze1
sentence-final particle meaning 'only', 'just', or downplaying something。
I am just sitting back eating peanuts (watching the drama unfold next to me).

5. Getting Fired: 炒魷魚caau2jau4jyu2
to get fired / lay off / fire someone

  • Literal meaning: "Stir-frying squid" (historically, when employees were fired from shops in Hong Kong, they had to pack up their sleeping mats, which rolled up like a curled, stir-fried squid).
  • Colloquial usage: Getting fired or firing someone.
  • Splitting example: When you want to say "the boss fired him," you split the verb 炒魷魚caau2jau4jyu2
    to get fired / lay off / fire someone     and insert the pronoun object (佢keoi5
    he / she / it    ) in the middle:
老闆lou5baan2
boss / proprietor 炒caau2
to stir-fry / speculate / fire 咗zo2
verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect) 佢keoi5
he / she / it 魷魚jau4jyu2
squid 喇喎laa3wo3
sentence-final particle combination indicating warning, sudden realization, or change of state！
Whoa, the boss fired him already!
