Chapter 21

Foodie Culture & Tea Restaurant Slang

Dive deep into the fast-paced foodie culture of Hong Kong. Learn how to customize your beverages like a local, master essential café shorthand, and use authentic slang for street-food hunting and late-night snacks.

In Hong Kong, food is not just a necessity; it is a way of life and a primary topic of conversation. Local diners, known as 茶餐廳caa4caan1teng1
Hong Kong style café / diner, operate at lightning speed. To keep up, waiters and customers use a rapid-fire shorthand.

In this chapter, you will learn how to customize your drinks (adjusting sweetness and ice), navigate local diner slang like ordering 茶走caa4zau2
milk tea with condensed milk instead of evaporated milk and sugar and toasted bread 烘底hung3dai2
toasted bread (restaurant slang), understand the social sharing practice of 搭枱daap3toi2
to share a table with strangers, and explore modern foodie culture terms like street-food hunting 掃街sou3gaai1
to go street food hunting / street-snacking and late-night eating 宵夜siu1je2
late night snack.

1. Beverage Customization: Controlling Sweetness & Ice

When ordering iced drinks in Hong Kong, they default to being quite sweet and heavily iced. If you want to drink like a true local, you must learn how to customize your order. This is done by adding short descriptors directly after the beverage name.

Sweetness Levels

  • 少甜siu2tim4
    less sweet    : Reduces the sugar syrup.
  • 走甜zau2tim4
    no sugar / unsweetened    : Removes the sugar entirely (literally "go sweetness").

Ice Levels

  • 少冰siu2bing1
    less ice    : Less ice, so your drink does not get watered down too quickly.
  • 走冰zau2bing1
    no ice    : Serves the drink chilled but with all ice removed.

2. Café Shorthand & Table Etiquette

In a crowded 茶餐廳caa4caan1teng1
Hong Kong style café / diner, space is premium and efficiency is king. Waiters use compact jargon to save time, and customers are expected to cooperate with the environment.

The Sweet Upgrade: 茶走caa4zau2
milk tea with condensed milk instead of evaporated milk and sugar

If you order a standard 奶茶naai5caa4
milk tea (typically Hong Kong-style), it is made with evaporated milk and white sugar. However, if you order 茶走caa4zau2
milk tea with condensed milk instead of evaporated milk and sugar, the waiter "sends away" (走zau2
to leave/remove) the white sugar and replaces it with thick, sweet condensed milk poured at the bottom of the glass. This makes the milk tea incredibly rich, smooth, and naturally sweet.

Requesting Toast: 烘底hung3dai2
toasted bread (restaurant slang)

When ordering any sandwich (like an egg sandwich or a ham sandwich), it is normally served on soft, untoasted white bread. If you prefer the bread to be warm and crunchy, you can append 烘底hung3dai2
toasted bread (restaurant slang) (toast the bottom) to your order. Note that cafés usually charge a tiny premium (one or two dollars) for this extra step!

Sharing Tables: 搭枱daap3toi2
to share a table with strangers

During busy breakfast or lunch rushes, you will rarely get a private table. If you see empty seats at a table where other customers are already eating, the host or waiter will tell you to 搭枱daap3toi2
to share a table with strangers (join tables). This is a perfectly normal, polite, and expected part of local dining etiquette. You simply sit down, order your food, and eat peacefully without needing to converse with the strangers next to you.

Dim Sum Culture: 飲茶jam2caa4
to drink tea / eat dim sum and 點心dim2sam1
dim sum

While cafés are for fast-paced meals, traditional Cantonese restaurants are for 飲茶jam2caa4
to drink tea / eat dim sum. This literally means "drinking tea", but it is the standard term for eating 點心dim2sam1
dim sum.

The Teapot Shorthand: 加gaa1
add 水seoi2
water

When your teapot is empty, you don't need to shout for a waiter. The universal silent request for a refill is to take the lid off the teapot and leave it resting half-open or on the table. If you must ask, you say 加gaa1
add 水seoi2
water to the waiter.

Tapping Fingers

When someone pours tea for you, it is customary to tap your index and middle fingers lightly on the table a few times. This is a silent way to say "thank you" without interrupting the ongoing conversation.

3. Spoken Foodie Culture: Street-Snacking, Late-Night Feasts & Social Media

Hong Kong's passion for food extends far beyond diners. The colloquial language has specific terms for how people socialise around food.

Street Snacking: 掃街sou3gaai1
to go street food hunting / street-snacking

Literally translating to "sweeping the street," 掃街sou3gaai1
to go street food hunting / street-snacking means going on a street-food crawl. Instead of sitting down for a single formal meal, you walk down busy streets (like in Mong Kok), hopping from stall to stall to buy curry fishballs, egg waffles, stinky tofu, and skewers.

The Sacred Fourth Meal: 宵夜siu1je2
late night snack

In a city that never sleeps, dinner is rarely the final meal. 宵夜siu1je2
late night snack refers to a late-night meal eaten with friends or family, usually between 10 PM and 2 AM. Common options include claypot rice, hot pot, local desserts, or dim sum at midnight.

Modern Rituals: 相機食先soeng2gei1sik6sin1
camera eats first (taking pictures of food before eating) & 打卡daa2kaa1
to check-in / take photos at trendy spots

Before anyone takes a bite of their beautifully presented food, someone will inevitably shout 相機食先soeng2gei1sik6sin1
camera eats first (taking pictures of food before eating)! It is a humorous modern expression representing the cultural habit of photographing dishes for social media.

This is closely related to 打卡daa2kaa1
to check-in / take photos at trendy spots. Originally meaning to punch a timecard at work, 打卡daa2kaa1
to check-in / take photos at trendy spots is now the colloquial verb for visiting trendy cafés, restaurants, or scenic spots specifically to take aesthetic photos and check-in online.
