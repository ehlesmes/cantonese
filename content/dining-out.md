Now that you can handle convenience store transactions and count currency, it is
time to experience the culinary heart of Hong Kong: the iconic
`茶餐廳[caa4caan1teng1|Hong Kong style café / diner]`.

A `茶餐廳[caa4caan1teng1|Hong Kong style café / diner]` is a fast-paced,
high-efficiency neighborhood eatery serving local comfort foods, sweet pastries,
and unique fusion drinks. In this lesson, you will learn to order food like a
local, specify options like hot or cold using
`定[ding6|or (used exclusively in alternative questions)]`, count dishes using
classifiers like `杯[bui1|cup / glass / mug (classifier for drinks)]` and
`碟[dip6|plate / dish (classifier for food dishes)]`, grasp the hidden menu
slang `靚仔[leng3zai2|handsome boy / plain white rice (restaurant slang)]`, and
use the perfective aspect marker
`咗[zo2|verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect)]`
to talk about what you have already eaten.

## 1. The Diner Lexicon: Core Drinks & Snacks

To get started at a `茶餐廳[caa4caan1teng1|Hong Kong style café / diner]`, you
must know the core staples. These items are loved by locals and represent the
fusion food culture of Hong Kong:

- `奶茶[naai5caa4|milk tea (typically Hong Kong-style)]`: Creamy, strong black
  tea filtered through a sack and sweetened with evaporated milk.
- `咖啡[gaa3fe1|coffee (loanword)]`: Strong local-style coffee.
- `鴛鴦[jyun1joeng1|yuanyang (drink mixing coffee and milk tea)]`: An iconic
  blend of seven parts milk tea and three parts coffee.
- `檸茶[leng4caa4|lemon tea]`: Black tea served with fresh slices of lemon.
- `菠蘿包[bo1lo1baau1|pineapple bun (sweet crusted bun, usually containing no pineapple)]`:
  A sweet, fluffy bun topped with a crunchy, sugary crust resembling a pineapple
  skin.
- `西多士[sai1do1si2|French toast (loanword)]`: Thick-sliced bread filled with
  peanut butter, dipped in egg, deep-fried, and served with butter and syrup.
- `蛋撻[daan6taat1|egg tart]`: A sweet custard pastry encased in a flaky puff
  pastry or crumbly shortcrust shell.

Let's look at how to express simple cravings using our verbs
`想[soeng2|to want to / would like to / think]` and
`要[jiu3|to want / need / must / demand]`:

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I / me] 想[soeng2|to want to / would like to / think] 飲[jam2|to drink] 奶茶[naai5caa4|milk tea (typically Hong Kong-style)] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
I would like to drink milk tea.
```

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I / me] 要[jiu3|to want / need / must / demand] 菠蘿包[bo1lo1baau1|pineapple bun (sweet crusted bun, usually containing no pineapple)] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]！
===
I want a pineapple bun!
```

## 2. Cold or Hot? Expressing Choices with `定[ding6|or (used exclusively in alternative questions)]`

When you order drinks in a Cantonese café, the server will almost always ask if
you want it hot or cold.

### Core Adjectives

- `熱[jit6|hot]`: Hot temperature (for drinks or food).
- `凍[dung3|cold / iced]`: Cold or iced temperature.

### Alternative Questions with `定[ding6|or (used exclusively in alternative questions)]`

To ask a question offering two alternatives, Cantonese uses the conjunction
`定[ding6|or (used exclusively in alternative questions)]`. Unlike the Mandarin
word for "or" (_huòzhě_),
`定[ding6|or (used exclusively in alternative questions)]` is reserved
**exclusively** for questions where the listener must make a choice between
option A and option B:

```cantonese
熱[jit6|hot] 定[ding6|or (used exclusively in alternative questions)] 凍[dung3|cold / iced] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]？
===
Hot or cold?
```

You can use `定[ding6|or (used exclusively in alternative questions)]` to
connect complete noun phrases or full actions:

```cantonese
你[nei5|you] 想[soeng2|to want to / would like to / think] 飲[jam2|to drink] 咖啡[gaa3fe1|coffee (loanword)] 定[ding6|or (used exclusively in alternative questions)] 檸茶[leng4caa4|lemon tea] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]？
===
Do you want to drink coffee or lemon tea?
```

```cantonese
呢個[ni1go3|this one] 係[hai6|to be (am/is/are) / yes] 菠蘿包[bo1lo1baau1|pineapple bun (sweet crusted bun, usually containing no pineapple)] 定[ding6|or (used exclusively in alternative questions)] 蛋撻[daan6taat1|egg tart] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]？
===
Is this a pineapple bun or an egg tart?
```

## 3. Counting Foods & Drinks: Classifiers

In Cantonese, you cannot simply place a number directly before a noun. You must
insert a **classifier** (also known as a measure word) between the number and
the noun. Choosing the right classifier depends on the shape or nature of the
item:

### Key Classifiers

- `個[go3|general measure word / individual / unit]`: The general, round-item
  classifier. Used for buns, tarts, and general round foods (e.g.,
  `菠蘿包[bo1lo1baau1|pineapple bun (sweet crusted bun, usually containing no pineapple)]`,
  `蛋撻[daan6taat1|egg tart]`).
- `杯[bui1|cup / glass / mug (classifier for drinks)]`: The container classifier
  for liquids. Used for any glass, cup, or mug of drink (e.g.,
  `奶茶[naai5caa4|milk tea (typically Hong Kong-style)]`,
  `咖啡[gaa3fe1|coffee (loanword)]`).
- `碟[dip6|plate / dish (classifier for food dishes)]`: The flat-dish
  classifier. Used for plates of food, such as stir-fries or barbecued meats
  (e.g., `叉燒[caa1siu1|barbecued pork / char siu]`).

### The Two Rules: `二[ji6|two]` vs `兩[loeng5|two / pair (used with classifiers)]`

Recall from Chapter 2 that when counting items using a classifier, the number
"two" must be spoken as `兩[loeng5|two / pair (used with classifiers)]`, never
as `二[ji6|two]`:

- **Correct**: `兩[loeng5|two / pair (used with classifiers)]`
  `杯[bui1|cup / glass / mug (classifier for drinks)]` (two cups)
- **Incorrect**: `二[ji6|two]`
  `杯[bui1|cup / glass / mug (classifier for drinks)]`

Let's look at full ordering examples combining quantities, temperatures, and
foods:

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I / me] 想[soeng2|to want to / would like to / think] 要[jiu3|to want / need / must / demand] 一[jat1|one] 杯[bui1|cup / glass / mug (classifier for drinks)] 凍[dung3|cold / iced] 檸茶[leng4caa4|lemon tea] 同埋[tung4maai4|and / with / together with] 兩[loeng5|two / pair (used with classifiers)] 個[go3|general measure word / individual / unit] 蛋撻[daan6taat1|egg tart] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
I would like to have a glass of iced lemon tea and two egg tarts.
```

```cantonese
唔該[m4goi1|excuse me / please / thank you (for service or help)]，我[ngo5|I / me] 要[jiu3|to want / need / must / demand] 一[jat1|one] 碟[dip6|plate / dish (classifier for food dishes)] 叉燒[caa1siu1|barbecued pork / char siu] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]。
===
Please, I need a plate of barbecued pork.
```

## 4. Polite Expressions & Request Softeners: `唔該[m4goi1|excuse me / please / thank you (for service or help)]` vs `多謝[do1ze6|thank you (for a gift, compliment, or hospitality)]` and the particle `啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]`

Being polite is essential when dining out. Cantonese has two distinct ways to
say "thank you," and their usage depends entirely on the situation.

### 1. The Gratitude Divide: `唔該[m4goi1|excuse me / please / thank you (for service or help)]` vs `多謝[do1ze6|thank you (for a gift, compliment, or hospitality)]`

- **`唔該[m4goi1|excuse me / please / thank you (for service or help)]`**: Used
  to thank someone for a **service, assistance, or favor** (e.g., when a waiter
  brings you water, clears your table, or gives you the bill). It also means
  "please" or "excuse me" when starting a request.
- **`多謝[do1ze6|thank you (for a gift, compliment, or hospitality)]`**: Used to
  thank someone for a **gift, compliment, money, or hospitality** (e.g., when a
  friend buys you lunch, gives you a present, or compliments your Cantonese). In
  retail, clerks will say
  `多謝[do1ze6|thank you (for a gift, compliment, or hospitality)]` to thank you
  for your payment or business.

Observe how they are used at the table:

```cantonese
唔該[m4goi1|excuse me / please / thank you (for service or help)]，我[ngo5|I / me] 想[soeng2|to want to / would like to / think] 埋單[maai5daan1|to pay the bill] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
Excuse me, I would like to pay the bill.
```

```cantonese
多謝[do1ze6|thank you (for a gift, compliment, or hospitality)] 你[nei5|you] 嘅[ge3|possessive particle] 蛋撻[daan6taat1|egg tart] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]！
===
Thank you for your egg tart!
```

### 2. Softening Suggestions and Requests: Particle `啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]`

The sentence-final particle
`啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]`
is used to soften commands, make suggestions, or encourage action. Without it, a
request can sound blunt or demanding. Adding
`啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]`
turns it into a polite request:

```cantonese
唔該[m4goi1|excuse me / please / thank you (for service or help)] 俾[bei2|to give] 一[jat1|one] 杯[bui1|cup / glass / mug (classifier for drinks)] 凍[dung3|cold / iced] 奶茶[naai5caa4|milk tea] 我[ngo5|I / me] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]。
===
Please give me a glass of iced milk tea.
```

## 5. Local Café Slang: Ordering a `靚仔[leng3zai2|handsome boy / plain white rice (restaurant slang)]`

If you listen closely to a busy café waiter shouting orders to the kitchen, you
might hear them call out
`靚仔[leng3zai2|handsome boy / plain white rice (restaurant slang)]`.

In daily life,
`靚仔[leng3zai2|handsome boy / plain white rice (restaurant slang)]` translates
literally to "handsome guy" or "handsome boy", and is often used to greet male
customers or friends. However, inside a
`茶餐廳[caa4caan1teng1|Hong Kong style café / diner]`,
`靚仔[leng3zai2|handsome boy / plain white rice (restaurant slang)]` has a
secondary double-meaning: it is the universal restaurant slang for a **bowl of
plain white rice** (because it is pure white and clean).

To order a plate of char siu with white rice, you can naturally say:

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I / me] 要[jiu3|to want / need / must / demand] 一[jat1|one] 碟[dip6|plate / dish (classifier for food dishes)] 叉燒[caa1siu1|barbecued pork / char siu] 同埋[tung4maai4|and / with / together with] 一[jat1|one] 個[go3|general measure word / individual / unit] 靚仔[leng3zai2|handsome boy / plain white rice (restaurant slang)] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
I want a plate of barbecued pork and a bowl of plain white rice.
```

## 6. Completed Actions: Perfective Suffix `咗[zo2|verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect)]`

In Cantonese, verbs do not change their form to express past tense. Instead, we
attach suffixes directly to the verb to express the **aspect** of the action
(how the action relates to time).

To express that an action has been successfully **completed**, attach the
perfective aspect suffix
`咗[zo2|verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect)]`
(similar to Mandarin _le_) directly behind the verb:

- `食[sik6|to eat]` +
  `咗[zo2|verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect)]`
  (eaten / ate)
- `飲[jam2|to drink]` +
  `咗[zo2|verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect)]`
  (drunk / drank)

> [!IMPORTANT] **Word Order with `咗[zo2|completed action marker]`** When a verb
> takes an object, the perfective suffix `咗[zo2|completed action marker]` must
> stay firmly attached to the verb, coming _before_ the object. The structure is
> **Verb + `咗[zo2|completed action marker]` + Object**. For example, say
> `食[sik6|eat]` `咗[zo2|completed action marker]` `嘢食[je5sik6|food]` (ate
> food), never `食[sik6|eat]` `嘢食[je5sik6|food]`
> `咗[zo2|completed action marker]`.

### Stating Completed Events

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I / me] 飲[jam2|to drink] 咗[zo2|verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect)] 咖啡[gaa3fe1|coffee (loanword)] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]！
===
I have drunk coffee!
```

```cantonese
佢哋[keoi5dei6|they / them] 食[sik6|to eat] 咗[zo2|verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect)] 菠蘿包[bo1lo1baau1|pineapple bun (sweet crusted bun, usually containing no pineapple)] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]。
===
They have eaten the pineapple buns.
```

### Asking "Have you yet?"

To ask if someone has completed an action yet, we place the adverb
`未[mei6|not yet / have not]` at the end of the sentence, followed by the
friendly question particle
`呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]`:

```cantonese
你[nei5|you] 食[sik6|to eat] 咗[zo2|verbal aspect suffix indicating completed action (perfective aspect)] 嘢食[je5sik6|snacks / food / things to eat] 未[mei6|not yet / have not] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]？
===
Have you eaten food yet?
```

To answer that you have not eaten yet, you can simply reply:

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I / me] 未[mei6|not yet / have not] 食[sik6|to eat] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
I haven't eaten yet.
```

## 7. Basic Emotions & States

How do you feel during or after a long day? Here are some core adjectives to
describe your basic emotions and physical states:

- `開心[hoi1sam1|happy / joyous]`: Happy.
- `嬲[nau1|angry / mad]`: Angry.
- `攰[gui6|tired / exhausted]`: Tired.
- `悶[mun6|bored / boring / stuffy]`: Bored.

### The Intensifier `好[hou2|very]` + Adjective

In Chapter 1, you learned `好[hou2|very]` as the adjective for "good". However,
when `好[hou2|very]` is placed directly before another adjective, it acts as an
intensifier meaning "very". This is especially common when expressing states or
emotions.

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I / me] 好[hou2|very] 攰[gui6|tired / exhausted] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
I am very tired.
```

```cantonese
佢[keoi5|he / she / it] 好[hou2|very] 開心[hoi1sam1|happy / joyous] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]！
===
He is very happy!
```

```cantonese
好[hou2|very] 靚[leng3|beautiful / high quality] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]！
===
It is very beautiful!
```

### Mini-Dialogue: Expressing Feelings

Here is a short exchange illustrating these new words.

```dialog
A: 你[nei5|you] 好[hou2|very] 嬲[nau1|angry / mad] 呀[aa3|particle]？
   === Are you very angry?
B: 係[hai6|yes] 呀[aa3|particle]，我[ngo5|I] 好[hou2|very] 攰[gui6|tired / exhausted] 同埋[tung4maai4|and / with / together with] 好[hou2|very] 悶[mun6|bored / boring / stuffy] 呀[aa3|particle]。 我[ngo5|I] 想[soeng2|to want to] 食[sik6|to eat] 嘢食[je5sik6|food]。
   === Yes, I am very tired and very bored. I want to eat food.
A: 你[nei5|you] 想[soeng2|to want to] 食[sik6|to eat] 蛋撻[daan6taat1|egg tart] 定[ding6|or] 菠蘿包[bo1lo1baau1|pineapple bun] 呀[aa3|particle]？
   === Do you want to eat an egg tart or a pineapple bun?
B: 我[ngo5|I] 要[jiu3|to want] 兩[loeng5|two] 個[go3|unit] 蛋撻[daan6taat1|egg tart] 啦[laa1|particle]！
   === I want two egg tarts!
A: 搞掂[gaau2dim6|done] 啦[laa1|particle]！ 呢度[ni1dou6|here] 呀[aa3|particle]。
   === Done! Here it is.
B: 多謝[do1ze6|thank you]！ 我[ngo5|I] 而家[ji4gaa1|now] 好[hou2|very] 開心[hoi1sam1|happy / joyous] 呀[aa3|particle]！
   === Thank you! I am very happy now!
```

## 8. Situational Dialogue: A Lively Café Lunch

Here is a realistic dialogue between a customer (A) and a busy diner waiter (B)
in a bustling Hong Kong `茶餐廳[caa4caan1teng1|Hong Kong style café / diner]`.
Notice how they order, request changes, confirm actions, and call for the bill
using `埋單[maai5daan1|to pay the bill / get the check]`.

```dialog
A: 唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]！ 我[ngo5|I] 想[soeng2|to want to] 要[jiu3|to want] 一[jat1|one] 個[go3|unit] 菠蘿包[bo1lo1baau1|pineapple bun] 同埋[tung4maai4|and] 一[jat1|one] 杯[bui1|cup] 奶茶[naai5caa4|milk tea] 呀[aa3|particle]。
   === Excuse me! I would like one pineapple bun and one cup of milk tea.
B: 好[hou2|good] 呀[aa3|particle]。 奶茶[naai5caa4|milk tea] 要[jiu3|to want] 熱[jit6|hot] 定[ding6|or] 凍[dung3|cold] 呀[aa3|particle]？
   === Sure. Do you want the milk tea hot or cold?
A: 我[ngo5|I] 要[jiu3|to want] 凍[dung3|cold] 嘅[ge3|particle]。 唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]！
   === I want the cold one. Thank you!
B: 好[hou2|good]，一[jat1|one] 個[go3|unit] 菠蘿包[bo1lo1baau1|pineapple bun]，一[jat1|one] 杯[bui1|cup] 凍[dung3|cold] 奶茶[naai5caa4|milk tea]。
   === Okay, one pineapple bun, one cup of cold milk tea.
A: 唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]，我[ngo5|I] 嘅[ge3|particle] 菠蘿包[bo1lo1baau1|pineapple bun] 搞掂[gaau2dim6|done] 咗[zo2|completed] 未[mei6|not yet] 呀[aa3|particle]？
   === Excuse me, is my pineapple bun ready yet?
B: 搞掂[gaau2dim6|done] 咗[zo2|completed] 啦[laa1|particle]！ 呢度[ni1dou6|here] 呀[aa3|particle]，唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]！
   === It is ready! Here it is, thank you!
A: 好[hou2|very] 靚[leng3|beautiful / high quality] 呀[aa3|particle]！ 多謝[do1ze6|thank you]！
   === It looks very beautiful! Thank you!
B: 你[nei5|you] 飲[jam2|to drink] 咗[zo2|completed] 凍[dung3|cold] 奶茶[naai5caa4|milk tea] 未[mei6|not yet] 呀[aa3|particle]？
   === Have you drunk the cold milk tea yet?
A: 飲[jam2|to drink] 咗[zo2|completed] 啦[laa1|particle]，好飲[hou2jam2|delicious (for drinks)] 呀[aa3|particle]！
   === Yes, I drank it, it is delicious!
A: 唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]，我[ngo5|I] 想[soeng2|to want to] 埋單[maai5daan1|to pay the bill] 啦[laa1|particle]。 幾多[gei2do1|how much] 錢[cin2|money] 呀[aa3|particle]？
   === Excuse me, I want to pay the bill. How much is it?
B: 三十[saam1sap6|thirty] 蚊[man1|dollar] 啦[laa1|particle]，唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]。
   === It is thirty dollars, thank you.
A: 好[hou2|good] 呀[aa3|particle]，我[ngo5|I] 俾[bei2|to give] 現金[jin6gam1|cash] 啦[laa1|particle]。
   === Great, I will pay with cash.
B: 搞掂[gaau2dim6|done] 啦[laa1|particle]！ 拜拜[baai1baai3|bye-bye]！
   === All done! Bye-bye!
A: 拜拜[baai1baai3|bye-bye]！
   === Bye-bye!
```
