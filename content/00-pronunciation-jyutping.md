Welcome to spoken Cantonese! Unlike Mandarin, which has four tones and is
heavily standardized, Colloquial Cantonese has **six distinct tones** and
preserves ancient phonetic endings (consonant stops) that give the language its
unique rhythmic, staccato flavor.

In this foundational chapter, we will demystify the sound system using LSHK
Jyutping, the standard romanization system for Cantonese.

---

## 1. The Six Tones

Cantonese is a pitch-sensitive tonal language. The exact same syllable,
pronounced with a different pitch or contour, will have a completely different
meaning.

There are six standard tones, which can be categorized by their pitch heights
(High, Mid, Low) and their movements (Flat/Level vs. Rising vs. Falling):

- **Tone 1 (High Flat / 55)**: High level pitch. Sounds like saying "Aha!" in
  realization.
  - Example: `詩[si1|poem / poetry]`
- **Tone 2 (High Rising / 35)**: Starts mid, rises high. Sounds like asking a
  question: "What?"
  - Example: `史[si2|history]`
- **Tone 3 (Mid Flat / 33)**: Neutral, mid level. Sounds like stating a
  matter-of-fact: "Okay."
  - Example: `試[si3|to try / test]`
- **Tone 4 (Low Falling / 21)**: Low, dropping pitch. Sounds like grunting in
  disappointment: "Oh..."
  - Example: `時[si4|time / hour]`
- **Tone 5 (Low Rising / 23)**: Starts low, rises slightly. Sounds like a
  hesitant question: "Really?"
  - Example: `市[si5|market / city]`
- **Tone 6 (Low Flat / 22)**: Deep, flat level pitch. Sounds like a low,
  assertive monotone: "Yep."
  - Example: `事[si6|matter / business / affair]`

### The "si" Tone Series

To practice hearing the six tones sequentially, native speakers often recite the
classic "si" sequence. Practice repeating these six words in order:

```cantonese
詩[si1|poem] 史[si2|history] 試[si3|to try] 時[si4|time] 市[si5|market] 事[si6|matter]
===
Poem, history, to try, time, market, matter
```

## 2. Vowel Length Distinction: `aa` vs `a`

In Cantonese, the length of a vowel is semantically significant. This is
especially true for the letter **A**:

- **`aa`** is a **long vowel** (pronounced open and long, like the "ah" in
  "father").
- **`a`** is a **short vowel** (pronounced clipped and short, like the "u" in
  "cup").

If you pronounce a long vowel short, or vice versa, you will say a completely
different word:

### Long Vowel `aa`

- `街[gaai1|street]`: Pronounced with a long, open "ah" sound (rhymes with
  English "guy").
- `沙[saa1|sand]`: Pronounced with a long, drawn-out "sah" sound.

### Short Vowel `a`

- `雞[gai1|chicken]`: Pronounced with a short, clipped "uh-ee" sound (close to
  English "gay").
- `新[san1|new]`: Pronounced with a short, abrupt "suhn" sound.

Observe the vowel length contrast in this simple phrase:

```cantonese
街[gaai1|street] 雞[gai1|chicken]
===
Street chicken
```

## 3. Silent Stop Consonant Endings (`-p`, `-t`, `-k`)

In English, when a word ends in `p`, `t`, or `k` (e.g., "cup", "cat", "back"),
speakers naturally release a small puff of air at the end of the consonant.

In Cantonese, these final consonants are **silent stops**. You must form the
shape of the consonant with your mouth, but **hold the air and do not release
it**:

- **`-p` Ending**: Close your lips fully to block the air, but do not make a
  "puh" sound.
  - Example: `十[sap6|ten]` (Pronounce "suh..." and seal your lips shut
    immediately).
- **`-t` Ending**: Place your tongue behind your teeth to block the air, but do
  not make a "tuh" sound.
  - Example: `日[jat6|day / sun]` in `今日[gam1jat6|today]`.
- **`-k` Ending**: Block the air at the back of your throat, but do not make a
  "kuh" sound.
  - Example: `食[sik6|to eat]`.

Practice pronouncing these unreleased stops naturally:

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I] 想[soeng2|want to] 食[sik6|to eat] 十[sap6|ten] 隻[zek3|classifier for animals] 雞[gai1|chicken]。
===
I want to eat ten chickens.
```

## 4. Spoken Exclamations: `喂[wai2|hey]` and `吓[haa2|what]`

Cantonese conversations are incredibly dynamic and expressive, filled with
emotional particles and exclamations that immediately convey the speaker's tone.
Two of the most high-frequency exclamations are:

1.  `喂[wai2|hey]`: Used to grab someone's attention, answer the phone
    ("hello?"), or express mild, friendly annoyance.
2.  `吓[haa2|what]`: Used to express surprise, confusion, disbelief, or to ask
    someone to repeat themselves.

Observe how these are naturally integrated in a conversational exchange:

```dialog
A: 喂[wai2|hey]，你[nei5|you] 食[sik6|to eat] 唔[m4|not] 食[sik6|to eat] 雞[gai1|chicken] 呀[aa3|particle]？
   === Hey, do you eat chicken?
B: 吓[haa2|what]？ 唔[m4|not] 食[sik6|to eat] 啦[laa1|particle]，我[ngo5|I] 唔[m4|not] 食[sik6|to eat] 事[si6|matter] 嘅[ge3|particle]。
   === What? No, I don't eat it.
```

## 5. Exercises

Test your understanding of the tones, vowel lengths, and stop endings introduced
in this chapter:

```exercise
question: |
  Which tone number represents the Low Falling pitch contour (e.g. `時[si4|time]`)?
  A) Tone 1
  B) Tone 3
  | C) Tone 4
  D) Tone 6
answer: C
explanation: Tone 4 is the Low Falling tone (pitch contour 21), which sounds like a disappointed grunt "oh...".
```

```exercise
question: |
  What is the main pronunciation difference between `街[gaai1|street]` and `雞[gai1|chicken]`?
  A) `街[gaai1|street]` has a silent stop ending, while `雞[gai1|chicken]` does not.
  B) `街[gaai1|street]` is pronounced with a long open vowel (aa), while `雞[gai1|chicken]` is pronounced with a short clipped vowel (a).
  C) They have different tones.
  D) `街[gaai1|street]` starts with a different initial consonant than `雞[gai1|chicken]`.
answer: B
explanation: `街[gaai1|street]` and `雞[gai1|chicken]` have the same initial and tone, but differ in vowel length: `街[gaai1|street]` uses the long open vowel `aa`, whereas `雞[gai1|chicken]` uses the short clipped vowel `a`.
```

```exercise
question: |
  Translate the exclamation "Hey!" used to get someone's attention in Cantonese:
answer: 喂[wai2|hey]
explanation: 喂[wai2|hey] is the standard conversational exclamation to call someone or grab their attention.
```
