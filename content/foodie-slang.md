In Hong Kong, food is not just a necessity; it is a way of life and a primary
topic of conversation. Local diners, known as
`茶餐廳[caa4caan1teng1|Hong Kong style café / diner]`, operate at lightning
speed. To keep up, waiters and customers use a rapid-fire shorthand.

In this chapter, you will learn how to customize your drinks (adjusting
sweetness and ice), navigate local diner slang like ordering
`茶走[caa4zau2|milk tea with condensed milk instead of evaporated milk and sugar]`
and toasted bread `烘底[hung3dai2|toasted bread (restaurant slang)]`, understand
the social sharing practice of
`搭枱[daap3toi2|to share a table with strangers]`, and explore modern foodie
culture terms like street-food hunting
`掃街[sou3gaai1|to go street food hunting / street-snacking]` and late-night
eating `宵夜[siu1je2|late night snack]`.

## 1. Beverage Customization: Controlling Sweetness & Ice

When ordering iced drinks in Hong Kong, they default to being quite sweet and
heavily iced. If you want to drink like a true local, you must learn how to
customize your order. This is done by adding short descriptors directly after
the beverage name.

### Sweetness Levels

- `少甜[siu2tim4|less sweet]`: Reduces the sugar syrup.
- `走甜[zau2tim4|no sugar / unsweetened]`: Removes the sugar entirely (literally
  "go sweetness").

### Ice Levels

- `少冰[siu2bing1|less ice]`: Less ice, so your drink does not get watered down
  too quickly.
- `走冰[zau2bing1|no ice]`: Serves the drink chilled but with all ice removed.

Let's see how these descriptors are placed directly after the drink name. There
is no need for extra conjunctions, and you can even stack these modifiers
sequentially (e.g., `少[siu2|less]` `甜[tim4|sweet]` `少[siu2|less]`
`冰[bing1|ice]`):

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I / me] 想[soeng2|to want to / would like to / think] 要[jiu3|to want / need / must / demand] 一[jat1|one] 杯[bui1|cup / glass / mug (classifier for drinks)] 凍檸茶[dung3leng4caa4|iced lemon tea] 少甜[siu2tim4|less sweet] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
I would like to have a glass of iced lemon tea, less sweet.
```

```cantonese
佢[keoi5|he / she] 要[jiu3|to want / need / must / demand] 凍[dung3|cold / iced] 咖啡[gaa3fe1|coffee (loanword)] 走甜[zau2tim4|no sugar / unsweetened] 走冰[zau2bing1|no ice] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]。
===
She wants an iced coffee, unsweetened and without ice.
```

## 2. Café Shorthand & Table Etiquette

In a crowded `茶餐廳[caa4caan1teng1|Hong Kong style café / diner]`, space is
premium and efficiency is king. Waiters use compact jargon to save time, and
customers are expected to cooperate with the environment.

### The Sweet Upgrade: `茶走[caa4zau2|milk tea with condensed milk instead of evaporated milk and sugar]`

If you order a standard `奶茶[naai5caa4|milk tea (typically Hong Kong-style)]`,
it is made with evaporated milk and white sugar. However, if you order
`茶走[caa4zau2|milk tea with condensed milk instead of evaporated milk and sugar]`,
the waiter "sends away" (`走[zau2|to leave/remove]`) the white sugar and
replaces it with thick, sweet condensed milk poured at the bottom of the glass.
This makes the milk tea incredibly rich, smooth, and naturally sweet.

### Requesting Toast: `烘底[hung3dai2|toasted bread (restaurant slang)]`

When ordering any sandwich (like an egg sandwich or a ham sandwich), it is
normally served on soft, untoasted white bread. If you prefer the bread to be
warm and crunchy, you can append
`烘底[hung3dai2|toasted bread (restaurant slang)]` (toast the bottom) to your
order. Note that cafés usually charge a tiny premium (one or two dollars) for
this extra step!

### Sharing Tables: `搭枱[daap3toi2|to share a table with strangers]`

During busy breakfast or lunch rushes, you will rarely get a private table. If
you see empty seats at a table where other customers are already eating, the
host or waiter will tell you to
`搭枱[daap3toi2|to share a table with strangers]` (join tables). This is a
perfectly normal, polite, and expected part of local dining etiquette. You
simply sit down, order your food, and eat peacefully without needing to converse
with the strangers next to you.

Let's look at these terms in action:

```cantonese
唔該[m4goi1|excuse me / please / thank you (for service or help)]，我[ngo5|I / me] 要[jiu3|to want / need / must / demand] 一[jat1|one] 杯[bui1|cup / glass / mug (classifier for drinks)] 凍[dung3|cold / iced] 茶走[caa4zau2|milk tea with condensed milk instead of evaporated milk and sugar] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
Excuse me, please, I want a glass of iced milk tea with condensed milk.
```

```cantonese
我哋[ngo5dei6|we / us] 喺[hai2|at / in / on] 呢度[ni1dou6|here] 搭枱[daap3toi2|to share a table with strangers] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]。
===
Let's share a table here.
```

### Dim Sum Culture: `飲茶[jam2caa4|to drink tea / eat dim sum]` and `點心[dim2sam1|dim sum]`

While cafés are for fast-paced meals, traditional Cantonese restaurants are for
`飲茶[jam2caa4|to drink tea / eat dim sum]`. This literally means "drinking
tea", but it is the standard term for eating `點心[dim2sam1|dim sum]`.

### The Teapot Shorthand: `加[gaa1|add]` `水[seoi2|water]`

When your teapot is empty, you don't need to shout for a waiter. The universal
silent request for a refill is to take the lid off the teapot and leave it
resting half-open or on the table. If you must ask, you say `加[gaa1|add]`
`水[seoi2|water]` to the waiter.

### Tapping Fingers

When someone pours tea for you, it is customary to tap your index and middle
fingers lightly on the table a few times. This is a silent way to say "thank
you" without interrupting the ongoing conversation.

## 3. Spoken Foodie Culture: Street-Snacking, Late-Night Feasts & Social Media

Hong Kong's passion for food extends far beyond diners. The colloquial language
has specific terms for how people socialise around food.

### Street Snacking: `掃街[sou3gaai1|to go street food hunting / street-snacking]`

Literally translating to "sweeping the street,"
`掃街[sou3gaai1|to go street food hunting / street-snacking]` means going on a
street-food crawl. Instead of sitting down for a single formal meal, you walk
down busy streets (like in Mong Kok), hopping from stall to stall to buy curry
fishballs, egg waffles, stinky tofu, and skewers.

### The Sacred Fourth Meal: `宵夜[siu1je2|late night snack]`

In a city that never sleeps, dinner is rarely the final meal.
`宵夜[siu1je2|late night snack]` refers to a late-night meal eaten with friends
or family, usually between 10 PM and 2 AM. Common options include claypot rice,
hot pot, local desserts, or dim sum at midnight.

### Modern Rituals: `相機食先[soeng2gei1sik6sin1|camera eats first (taking pictures of food before eating)]` & `打卡[daa2kaa1|to check-in / take photos at trendy spots]`

Before anyone takes a bite of their beautifully presented food, someone will
inevitably shout
`相機食先[soeng2gei1sik6sin1|camera eats first (taking pictures of food before eating)]`!
It is a humorous modern expression representing the cultural habit of
photographing dishes for social media.

This is closely related to
`打卡[daa2kaa1|to check-in / take photos at trendy spots]`. Originally meaning
to punch a timecard at work,
`打卡[daa2kaa1|to check-in / take photos at trendy spots]` is now the colloquial
verb for visiting trendy cafés, restaurants, or scenic spots specifically to
take aesthetic photos and check-in online.

Let's see how these lifestyle terms are used in daily conversation:

```cantonese
我哋[ngo5dei6|we / us] 今日[gam1jat6|today] 去[heoi3|to go] 旺角[wong6gok3|Mong Kok] 掃街[sou3gaai1|to go street food hunting / street-snacking] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]！
===
Let's go street food hunting in Mong Kok today!
```

```cantonese
食[sik6|to eat] 嘢食[je5sik6|snacks / food / things to eat] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]！ 唔好[m4hou2|don't / should not] 相機食先[soeng2gei1sik6sin1|camera eats first (taking pictures of food before eating)] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]！
===
Eat the food! Don't let the camera eat first!
```

## 4. Situational Dialogue: A Busy Café & A Foodie Plan

Let's join two friends, Ka-Ho and Ching-Yee, as they meet for a quick lunch at a
crowded diner, customize their drinks, navigate diner seating, and plan a
weekend street-food excursion.

```dialog
A: 唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]！ 呢度[ni1dou6|here] 有冇[jau5mou5|is there / have or not] 兩[loeng5|two] 個[go3|unit] 位[wai2|seat] 呀[aa3|particle]？
   === Excuse me! Are there two seats here?
B: 喺[hai2|at] 嗰度[go2dou6|there] 搭枱[daap3toi2|share table] 啦[laa1|particle]，唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]。
   === Please share a table over there, thank you.
A: 好[hou2|good] 呀[aa3|particle]，我哋[ngo5dei6|we] 喺[hai2|at] 呢度[ni1dou6|here] 坐[co5|sit] 啦[laa1|particle]。 你[nei5|you] 想[soeng2|want to] 飲[jam2|drink] 咩嘢[me1je5|what] 呀[aa3|particle]？
   === Sure, let's sit here. What do you want to drink?
C: 我[ngo5|I] 想[soeng2|want to] 飲[jam2|drink] 凍檸茶[dung3leng4caa4|iced lemon tea] 少甜[siu2tim4|less sweet] 呀[aa3|particle]。
   === I want to drink iced lemon tea, less sweet.
A: 我[ngo5|I] 要[jiu3|want] 凍[dung3|iced] 茶走[caa4zau2|tea with condensed milk] 呀[aa3|particle]，唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]。 伙記[fo2gei3|waiter]！ 我哋[ngo5dei6|we] 要[jiu3|want] 一[jat1|one] 杯[bui1|cup] 凍檸茶[dung3leng4caa4|iced lemon tea] 少甜[siu2tim4|less sweet] 同埋[tung4maai4|and] 一[jat1|one] 杯[bui1|cup] 凍[dung3|iced] 茶走[caa4zau2|tea with condensed milk]。
   === I want iced milk tea with condensed milk, thank you. Waiter! We want a glass of iced lemon tea less sweet and a glass of iced milk tea with condensed milk.
B: 搞掂[gaau2dim6|done] 咗[zo2|completed] 啦[laa1|particle]！ 呢度[ni1dou6|here] 呀[aa3|particle]，唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]。
   === Ready! Here they are, thank you.

   === Wow, it looks so nice! Wait a moment, camera eats first!
A: 你[nei5|you] 真係[zan1hai6|really] 鍾意[zung1ji3|like] 打卡[daa2kaa1|check-in] 呀[aa3|particle]！
   === You really like taking photos for social media!
C: 係[hai6|yes] 呀[aa3|particle]！ 我哋[ngo5dei6|we] 今日[gam1jat6|today] 飲[jam2|drink] 完[jyun4|finish] 嘢飲[je5jam2|drinks]，第日[dai6jat6|another day] 去[heoi3|go] 掃街[sou3gaai1|street food hunt] 玩[waan2|play] 啦[laa1|particle]！
   === Yes! After we finish our drinks today, let's go street-food hunting another day!
A: 好[hou2|good] 呀[aa3|particle]，我哋[ngo5dei6|we] 去[heoi3|go] 掃街[sou3gaai1|street food hunt] 食[sik6|eat] 宵夜[siu1je2|late night snack] 啦[laa1|particle]！ 唔該[m4goi1|excuse me]，埋單[maai5daan1|pay bill] 啦[laa1|particle]！
   === Great, let's go street-food hunting and eat late-night snacks! Excuse me, bill please!
```
