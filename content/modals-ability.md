In this chapter, we will learn how to express different types of capability and
permission using Cantonese modal verbs, and how to use potential suffixes
directly with verbs to indicate physical feasibility.

Let's dive in!

---

## 1. The Three Faces of "Can": `識[sik1|to know how to]`, `可以[ho2ji5|can]`, and `會[wui5|will / can]`

In English, we use the word "can" to cover many meanings. In Cantonese, you must
choose the correct modal verb based on the _type_ of capability.

### 1. Learned Skills and Abilities: `識[sik1|to know how to]`

Use `識[sik1|to know how to]` when "can" means you **know how to do** something
because you learned it. This is used for languages, sports, driving, cooking,
and academic subjects.

- `我[ngo5|I]` `識[sik1|to know how to]` `講[gong2|to speak]`
  `廣東話[gwong2dung1waa2|Cantonese language]` — I can (know how to) speak
  Cantonese.
- `我[ngo5|I]` `識[sik1|to know how to]` `講[gong2|to speak]`
  `英文[jing1man4|English language]` — I can (know how to) speak English.
- `我[ngo5|I]` `識[sik1|to know how to]` `揸車[zaa1ce1|to drive a car]` — I can
  (know how to) drive a car.

To negate this, place `唔[m4|not]` before `識[sik1|to know how to]`:

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I] 唔[m4|not] 識[sik1|to know how to] 寫[se2|to write] 英文[jing1man4|English language] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
I do not know how to write English.
```

### 2. Situational Permission and Possibility: `可以[ho2ji5|can]`

Use `可以[ho2ji5|can]` when "can" means you are **permitted** to do something or
it is **situationally possible** (e.g. schedules align).

- `你[nei5|you]` `可以[ho2ji5|can]` `喺[hai2|at / in / on]` `呢度[ni1dou6|here]`
  `飲[jam2|drink]` `咖啡[gaa3fe1|coffee]` — You can (are allowed to) drink
  coffee here.
- `我[ngo5|I]` `今日[gam1jat6|today]` `可以[ho2ji5|can]` `去[heoi3|go]`
  `旅行[leoi5hang4|to travel]` — I can (am able to situationally) go travel
  today.

To negate permission or situational ability, place `唔[m4|not]` before
`可以[ho2ji5|can]`:

```cantonese
呢度[ni1dou6|here] 唔[m4|not] 可以[ho2ji5|can] 揸車[zaa1ce1|to drive a car] 囉[lo1|sentence-final particle for obviousness]！
===
You cannot drive a car here, obviously!
```

### 3. Future Likelihood and Tendencies: `會[wui5|will / can]`

Use `會[wui5|will / can]` to express **future actions** ("will") or an
**inherent likelihood/tendency** that something will happen.

- `我[ngo5|I]` `聽日[ting1jat6|tomorrow]` `會[wui5|will / can]`
  `嚟[lei4|to come / sentence-final particle]` — I will come tomorrow.

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I] 唔[m4|not] 會[wui5|will / can] 去[heoi3|go] 日本[jat6bun2|Japan] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]。
===
I will not go to Japan.
```

## 2. Physical Feasibility and Obstacles: Potential Suffixes `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]`, `唔得[m4dak1|cannot]`, and `唔到[m4dou2|cannot]`

In daily spoken Cantonese, instead of using helper words like
`可以[ho2ji5|can]`, native speakers prefer to attach suffixes directly to the
end of verbs to show whether an action is physically possible.

### 1. Capability & Physical Feasibility: `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]`

When you attach `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]` directly to the end of a verb, it
indicates that the action is **physically doable, allowed, or ready**:

> **Formula:** [Verb] + `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]`

- `食[sik6|to eat]` `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]` — edible / ready to eat. (e.g.
  `呢個[ni1go3|this one]` `食[sik6|to eat]` `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]` — This
  is ready to eat!)
- `去[heoi3|go]` `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]` — ready to go / able to go.
- `行[hang4|to walk]` `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]` — able to walk / ready to
  walk.

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I] 依家[ji1gaa1|now] 去[heoi3|go] 得[dak1|okay / can / ready] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]！
===
I can go now! / I am ready to go now!
```

### 2. Lack of Permission or Suitability: `唔[m4|not]`...`得[dak1|okay / can / ready]`

To express that an action is **not allowed, forbidden, or physically
bad/unsuitable to do**, place the negation particle `唔[m4|not]` before the
verb, followed by `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]`:

> **Formula:** `唔[m4|not]` + [Verb] + `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]`

- `唔[m4|not]` `食[sik6|to eat]` `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]` — not allowed to
  eat / shouldn't be eaten.
- `唔[m4|not]` `去[heoi3|go]` `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]` — not allowed to go.

### 3. Ability and Inability to Succeed: `到[dou2|successfully do (verb complement)]` and `唔到[m4dou2|cannot]`

When you want to describe whether you **successfully manage to** do something
(overcoming physical obstacles or reaching a result), you attach the potential
suffix `到[dou2|successfully do (verb complement)]` directly to the verb.
Conversely, if you **fail to manage** or **cannot** complete the action, you
attach `唔到[m4dou2|cannot]`:

- `睇[tai2|to look / watch]` `到[dou2|successfully do (verb complement)]` — able
  to see / successfully saw.
- `睇[tai2|to look / watch]` `唔到[m4dou2|cannot]` — unable to see (e.g.,
  someone is blocking you).
- `聽[teng1|to listen]` `到[dou2|successfully do (verb complement)]` — able to
  hear / successfully heard.
- `聽[teng1|to listen]` `唔到[m4dou2|cannot]` — unable to hear.
- `食[sik6|to eat]` `唔到[m4dou2|cannot]` — unable to eat (e.g., because you are
  too full or the restaurant is closed).
- `去[heoi3|go]` `唔到[m4dou2|cannot]` — unable to go.

> [!TIP] **`唔得[m4dak1|cannot]` vs `唔到[m4dou2|cannot]`**
>
> - Use the structure `唔[m4|not]` + [Verb] + `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]` when
>   an action is **not allowed** or **bad for you** (e.g., `唔[m4|not]`
>   `食[sik6|to eat]` `得[dak1|okay / can / ready]` means it's toxic, expired,
>   or forbidden to eat).
> - Use `唔到[m4dou2|cannot]` when you **fail to manage** or **physically
>   cannot** complete the action (e.g., `食[sik6|to eat]` `唔到[m4dou2|cannot]`
>   means you want to eat it, but you are too full, have no teeth, or the
>   kitchen is closed).

Let's look at how this sounds in context:

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I] 聽日[ting1jat6|tomorrow] 去[heoi3|go] 唔到[m4dou2|cannot] 日本[jat6bun2|Japan] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
I cannot go to Japan tomorrow.
```

```cantonese
我[ngo5|I] 聽[teng1|to listen] 唔到[m4dou2|cannot] 你[nei5|you] 講[gong2|to speak] 咩嘢[me1je5|what kind of thing] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
===
I cannot hear what you are saying.
```

## 3. Conversational Dialogue

Below is a dialogue illustrating modals and potential suffixes.

```dialog
A: 你[nei5|you] 識唔識[sik1 m4 sik1|do you know how to?] 揸車[zaa1ce1|to drive a car] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]？
   === Do you know how to drive a car?
B: 我[ngo5|I] 識[sik1|to know how to] 揸車[zaa1ce1|to drive a car] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]，但係[daan6hai6|but] 我[ngo5|I] 今日[gam1jat6|today] 去[heoi3|go] 唔到[m4dou2|cannot] 嗰度[go2dou6|there] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
   === I know how to drive, but I cannot manage to go there today.
A: 點解[dim2gaai2|why] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]？ 呢度[ni1dou6|here] 唔[m4|not] 可以[ho2ji5|can] 揸車[zaa1ce1|to drive a car] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]？
   === Why? Are you not allowed to drive here?
B: 唔係[m4hai6|no / is not]，因為[jan1wai6|because] 塞車[sak1ce1|traffic jam] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]，我[ngo5|I] 行[hang4|to walk] 唔到[m4dou2|cannot] 囉[lo1|sentence-final particle for obviousness]！
   === No, because of a traffic jam, I cannot manage to move (walk/go), obviously!
A: 噉[gam2|like this / then] 你[nei5|you] 聽日[ting1jat6|tomorrow] 會唔會[wui5 m4 wui5|will or will not] 去[heoi3|go] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]？
   === Then will you go tomorrow?
B: 會[wui5|will / can] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]，聽日[ting1jat6|tomorrow] 去[heoi3|go] 得[dak1|okay / can / ready] 啦[laa1|sentence-final particle suggesting, requesting, or encouraging action]！
   === Yes, I can go tomorrow!
```

## 4. Exercises

Review the modals of ability and potential suffixes:

```exercise
question: |
  Which of the following sentences correctly translates "I can drive a car" (expressing a learned skill) into Cantonese?
  A) 我[ngo5|I] 可以[ho2ji5|can] 揸車[zaa1ce1|to drive a car] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
  B) 我[ngo5|I] 識[sik1|to know how to] 揸車[zaa1ce1|to drive a car] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
  C) 我[ngo5|I] 會[wui5|will / can] 揸車[zaa1ce1|to drive a car] 呀[aa3|sentence-final particle softening statements, making questions friendly]。
answer: B
explanation: 識[sik1|to know how to] is the modal verb used specifically for learned skills or abilities like driving. 可以[ho2ji5|can] refers to situational permission or possibility, and 會[wui5|will / can] expresses future likelihood or tendency.
```

```exercise
  Translate the following sentence into colloquial Cantonese with correct annotations: "I cannot hear what you are saying."

explanation: 聽[teng1|to listen] 唔到[m4dou2|cannot] is the potential suffix structure representing the physical inability to hear.
```
